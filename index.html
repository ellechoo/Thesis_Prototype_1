Accent

After you stop recording, a free open-source model (CommonAccent) compares your speech with 16 groups of English accents. It needs at least 5 seconds and uses the first 30.

Vowel space

What this shows: how you shape your mouth for vowel sounds, measured from the sound alone. Every dot is a split second of your voice. Dots high up mean your mouth was nearly closed; low means open. Left means your tongue was pushed forward; right means pulled back.

Together the dots form a cloud: your vowel space. The dashed shape is the average American English speaker, for comparison. It isn't a “correct” target.

Things to look for

  • The shape of your cloud. Ignore single dots, which include the slides between sounds. Look at how far the cloud stretches toward each corner.
  • Shifts from the dashed shape. Accents move vowels around. “Oo” sitting further left than the reference is common in California and southern England.
  • “Hot” and “coffee.” If they land in the same place, you say “cot” and “caught” the same way, like much of the US and Canada.
  • Higher voices sit lower and further left overall, because smaller mouths and throats resonate higher. That's why the reference switches with your pitch.
More about the vowel chart

Each faint dot is one moment of voiced speech (about 40 per second), placed by its first two formants. Up and down is F1, which follows how open your mouth is. Left and right is F2, which follows where your tongue is: front vowels like “heed” on the left, back vowels like “who'd” on the right.

Reading a passage mixes every vowel together, plus the glides between them, so the cloud is blurry by nature. Look at its overall shape and edges: where it reaches furthest up-left (your “ee”), down (your “ah”) and right (your “oo”), and how that compares with the dashed reference shape. Accents show up as shifts, for example “who'd”-type vowels sitting further left, or a lower, more open “had”.

The reference is a 1950s General American average, not a “correct” target. Higher-pitched voices make formants harder to measure, so expect more scatter.

How to read the accent result

The model was trained on volunteer recordings from Mozilla Common Voice. It sorts speech into 16 broad groups, such as “United States” or “England”. It can't tell regions within a country apart, and it has no group for many accents, so a mixed or regional accent gets matched to whichever group sounds most similar.

The percentages show how much the model leans toward each group. They aren't a measure of how “strong” an accent is. Background noise, a far-away mic or very short recordings make results less reliable. Try reading the passage twice and compare.

Accents say nothing about ability or intelligence. Treat this as a curiosity, not a judgment about a person.
