talking pause

Speaking rate is syllables per second over the whole reading, pauses included. Read-aloud English is often around 4 to 5.

Pauses are silences longer than 0.2 seconds. Everyone pauses a little at the commas and the period, so the interesting part is extra pauses, or unusually long ones. Shorter gaps are just consonants like the “t” in “truck”.

Vowel space

What this shows: how you shape your mouth for vowel sounds, measured from the sound alone. Every dot is a split second of your voice. Dots high up mean your mouth was nearly closed; low means open. Left means your tongue was pushed forward; right means pulled back.

Together the dots form a cloud: your vowel space. The dashed shape is the average American English speaker, for comparison. It isn't a “correct” target.

your voiced sounds (0) average American English speaker (Peterson & Barney, 1952)

Things to look for

  • The shape of your cloud. Ignore single dots, which include the slides between sounds. Look at how far the cloud stretches toward each corner.
  • Shifts from the dashed shape. Accents move vowels around. “Oo” sitting further left than the reference is common in California and southern England.
  • “Chocolate” and “small.” Many people in the US and Canada say these two vowels the same way (the cot–caught merger). If you do, the lower right of your cloud forms one blob instead of two.
  • Higher voices sit lower and further left overall, because smaller mouths and throats resonate higher. That's why the reference switches with your pitch.

More about the vowel chart

Each faint dot is one moment of voiced speech (about 40 per second), placed by its first two formants. Up and down is F1, which follows how open your mouth is. Left and right is F2, which follows where your tongue is.

Reading a passage mixes every vowel together, plus the glides between them, so the cloud is blurry by nature. The reference is a 1950s General American average, not a “correct” target. Higher-pitched voices make formants harder to measure, so expect more scatter.

How to read the accent result

The model (CommonAccent) was trained on volunteer recordings from Mozilla Common Voice. It sorts speech into 16 broad groups, such as “United States” or “England”. It can't tell regions within a country apart, and it has no group for many accents, so a mixed or regional accent gets matched to whichever group sounds most similar.

The percentages show how much the model leans toward each group. They aren't a measure of how “strong” an accent is. Background noise, a far-away mic or very short recordings make results less reliable.

Accents say nothing about ability or intelligence. Treat this as a curiosity, not a judgment about a person.
